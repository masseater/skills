# Specifics of designing rhythm

Apply these together with the norms in the other `references/`.

- Build sentences on the beat of "set up, flow, stop"
- Alternate dense and sparse paragraphs
- Create one unresolved tension at the opening
- Enter a section not with a declaration but with a counter-question, a sense of something off, or a confession
- Resolve questions explicitly, and land the ending on something concrete
- Tell release from bad writing by whether a sentence updates the situation or the document
- When finished, inspect in the order topic, leakage, tension ledger, beat, boundary

## The beat of sentences

Lay a foothold with a short sentence, flow with a longer sentence, and stop with a short sentence. Make "set up, flow, stop" the basic beat of a paragraph.

Do not push through with assertions alone. Alternate assertion and hesitation.

- Assertions are "it was ...", "it is ...", "that is how it is"
- Hesitations are "it must be ...", "I do think ... But ...", "could it be ...?"

Hesitation is not weakness but a device. A conviction that is later betrayed by the facts lays the groundwork for steering the reader's prediction and then breaking it. Write "It must be going well." and in the next paragraph break it with "But later, looking at the records, that was not the case."

At turning points, the beats of concession, turn and short stop can be used. As in "It would be ... It will continue to be ... But what we deal with here is ... That is what I want to think about.", the short directive sentence after the turn fixes the reader's gaze.

## The density waveform of paragraphs

When two or three dense paragraphs continue, put one sparse paragraph. The function of a sparse paragraph is limited to one of the following.

- Pinning down a settled matter in one line
- Presenting the next subject of judgment
- Switching the distance of the viewpoint

Do not fix the distance of the viewpoint. Alternate paragraphs that close in on the concrete, such as records, numbers, remarks and code, with paragraphs that step back one level to give meaning.

Bullet points can also be used as a pause that stops the breathing of the body. A sentence that steps back one level after an enumeration, such as "In short, ...", works because of this pause.

## Designing the opening

The job of the opening is to create one unresolved tension in the first few sentences. The form does not matter.

- Go from restating the reader's own feeling to a hypothesis
- Ask the reader a question directly and immediately give your own answer
- Put a general proposition with conviction, and test that conviction in the body that follows
- Write out the narrator's assumption affirmatively, then break it with facts
- Restate, in the words of a participant, a question left by the previous chapter or section

Previews and summaries may be used. A sentence with an attitude, such as "There should be no angle more fitting than this for thinking about ...", creates tension by itself. What to avoid is only an agenda with no attitude, such as "This chapter covers A, B and C". Give the preview an attitude, or put the handling of the reader's feelings or resistance before the preview.

Say the resistance readers are likely to have first, in the readers' words: that it is old, contrived, impractical, irrelevant to them, and so on. Deal with it briefly, then enter the main topic.

## How to enter a section

Do not declare "This section covers ..." at the head of a section. Enter with one of the following.

- Restate a sense of something off left by the previous section as a participant's question
- Write the counter-question readers would naturally have as it is. Do not answer immediately; take it once, then break it
- Enter with the writer's confession. Use the confession as a foothold for the argument that follows

Bring in theories, concepts and quotations as naming, not as answers, after creating in the reader "a sense of something off that has no name yet". Presenting the theory first and confirming it with examples robs readers of discovery.

Place the bridge between sections at the head of the next section, not at the end of the previous one. Adding a "next we look at ..." preview to the end of the previous section is narrating the proceedings, and is bad writing.

## Landing enumerations

After enumerating properties or categories, land each item one by one on a concrete scene just before. Write like "The first is the true nature of the ... we just saw" or "The second also rings a bell". Do not make the style of landing uniform. Vary it among pointing out the true nature, ringing a bell, mapping to a particular fact, and giving up on or changing the future.

## Resolving questions and the ending

Resolve explicitly the questions raised along the way. Returning a question half at a time, as in "This is half the answer" and "The other half lies in ...", gives momentum to the second half.

For the ending, land the accumulated abstractions on something concrete the reader already has, then close. The opening scene, the reader's own experience and the early questions are such things. Choose which tensions to close, and you may leave just one open at the end.

Second-person address, requests to the reader, and the writer's modesty or disclaimers work as release only at boundaries such as the opening or ending of a chapter. Do not mix them into the argument in the middle.

## Telling release from bad writing

There is only one axis of judgment: does the sentence update the situation, or the document?

- Sentences that update the situation may be kept as release
- Sentences that update the document are deleted in principle

Sentences that update the situation newly convey events of the subject world, data, people's remarks, or the state of the narrator's judgment. Assumptions, reservations, regrets, concessions and confessions are states of the narrator's judgment.

Sentences that update the document convey only how this chapter, this section or the story so far "looks", or "what will be written next".

### Typical bad writing

In all of them, the topic is the text itself, and there is no information about the situation.

- "So far it probably looks like an explanation of concepts. So let's go right back to the examples."
- "In short, the theme of this chapter is not ... but ..."
- "Don't misunderstand me; I don't mean to deny ..."
- "I will not list techniques." "This is not a story about ... The question is only ..."
- "Let me put half the answer first." "Let me draw the line one last time."
- "So far we have seen ... The next question is ..." "Next we look at ..."

Bad writing also appears in the form of short assertions. When a document-updating sentence is reshaped into a short assertive tone, it looks like a punchline and tends to survive. This is the biggest path by which bad writing gets in. Being short and rhythmic is not a reason to keep it.

### Typical good release

All of them update the situation or the state of the narrator's judgment.

- "It must be going well." is an assumption, groundwork to be broken later
- "Well, it's not something that needs action right now, but at some point it will need sorting out." is a reservation of judgment
- "It's frustrating that I would have done that if I had known from the start." is an emotion that makes visible an error of judgment
- "I do think I wanted to do that." is a concession to a counter-question, a foothold for the turn that follows

### Document-updating sentences that may be kept

Even sentences about the document may be kept in only the following four forms.

1. Sentences that quote and dismiss a specific misreading or objection by the reader. A sentence that only vaguely says "don't misunderstand me" is bad writing
2. Sentences that set up and resolve a question, placed at a boundary after tension has been created. A declaration of what the text is not is not setting up a question
3. Sentences of requests to the reader or disclaimers placed at a boundary
4. Sentences that open the frame of an imaginary example or scene, such as "Let's say ...", and close it, such as "Let's give the opening example its punchline"

### How to fix

1. Delete the sentence that only updates the document, and read what comes before and after. If it connects, you are done
2. If the logic jumps with the deletion, rewrite what that sentence was trying to point at as a sentence on the side of the situation
3. If the rewritten result still makes the text itself the topic, delete the whole sentence and rebuild the bridge between what comes before and after

"So far it looks like an explanation of concepts" becomes "All three of these properties are present in the failure at the opening."

## Inspection after writing

1. Topic test. Pick up every first sentence of a paragraph and every independent short sentence, and judge whether it updates the situation or the document
2. Leakage test. Search for whether vocabulary of these norms, such as "half the answer", "tension", "resolve" or "draw the line", appears in the body. If it does, that is evidence of declaring a device, so realize it again on the side of content
3. Tension ledger. List the questions, assumptions and promises you raised, and point to the line where each is resolved. For those you cannot point to, add a resolution or delete them
4. Beat inspection. Where three or more long assertive sentences continue, insert a short foothold or stop, or a hesitation
5. Boundary inspection. If second-person address, requests or modesty appear in the middle of the body, move them to a boundary or delete them

Sentences newly written or re-split short during revision are a path by which bad writing gets in. Put them through the topic test right after writing them too.

## Drawing the prescription from the symptom

| Symptom | Cause | Prescription |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Every paragraph has the same tone and it is tiring | No beat in the sentences | Apply the beat inspection |
| It is correct but there is no urge to keep reading | No unresolved tension | Create tension at the opening, and confirm with the tension ledger that one is always open |
| The temperature suddenly drops in the theory section | The theory is presented before the sense of something off | Put a counter-question or confession before the theory, and land enumerations on scenes |
| There are loose sentences, yet it looks slack | The release has become narration of the proceedings | Put it through the topic test, and rewrite it as a sentence on the side of the situation |
| The end of the chapter is preachy | It closes while still abstract | Land it on something concrete the reader has, and leave one undecided question |
| The opening is businesslike | It has become an agenda with no attitude | Give the preview an attitude, or put the handling of the reader's feelings and resistance first |
